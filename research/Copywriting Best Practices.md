# Copywriting Best Practices — CB Dejvice Fundraising Chain

**Scope:** email → 1-page brochure (PDF w/ QR) → landing page → donation CTA.
**Audience:** warm, in-network US contacts of missionaries and religious institutions.
**Compiled:** 2026-07-26. Downstream copywriters should treat this as the working brief.

---

## How to read this document

Two of the sources below are church/ministry-specific and are weighted heavily for **tone and the ask**. The rest are secular nonprofit/conversion research and are used **only for mechanics** — structure, length, placement, form design. Every section is split into **Mechanics to use** and **Tone to avoid** so the two never get blended by accident.

The governing constraint: this is a church asking friends to consider a gift. Where a conversion tactic and the humility constraint conflict, humility wins. Those conflicts are named explicitly below rather than left to judgement.

## 1. Cross-chain rules (apply to all four assets)

### Mechanics to use

- **Message match end to end.** The subject line, the email's core phrase, the brochure headline, and the landing page headline must repeat the same handful of words. NextAfter measured a 272% conversion lift and 420% revenue lift from aligning email and landing-page messaging alone (Experiment #7176). Pick one governing line — something in the family of *"After 30 years in a rented hotel, a permanent home for Prague 6"* — and let it recur verbatim.
- **Donor-centred grammar.** More "you," less "we/our/the church." NextAfter recorded an 85% conversion increase from shifting a page to "you" language (Experiment #16355). *Caveat below in "Tone to avoid" — this needs a specific Czech-church adaptation.*
- **One idea per asset.** Email sells the click. Brochure sells the vision. Landing page sells the gift. Do not attempt the full case three times.
- **Concrete numbers everywhere.** 1995. 109,000 residents. 38,000 students. 30 years. 54M CZK / $2.55M. 14M CZK already committed by members. 40M CZK / $1.89M remaining. Specificity is the single strongest trust signal available here and it is free — the source material is unusually rich in it. Always pair CZK with the USD equivalent for a US audience.
- **Short sentences, generous white space, bold subheads.** Abstract Union's capital-campaign guidance: condensed writing, short sentences, maximum negative space, skimmable throughout. Long sentences "interfere with your power to engage, persuade, and inspire."
- **Plain English over church jargon.** Both church sources are emphatic. Assume some readers are foundation staff or friends-of-friends without insider vocabulary. "Discipleship groups" is fine; unexplained insider shorthand is not.

### Tone to avoid

- **Urgency manufacture.** The secular research leans hard on artificial deadlines, countdowns, "this Christmas," scarcity. NextAfter's Experiment #641 got a 166.4% lift partly from strikethrough numbers and urgency language. **Do not use this.** There is a real timeline (permission granted, construction ahead) — state it plainly once and let it do its own work. No countdown clocks, no strikethroughs, no "act before it's too late."
- **ALL CAPS CTAs and exclamation marks.** NextAfter recommends caps for CTA buttons. Inappropriate here and reads as spam to this audience. Sentence case throughout.
- **Over-rotated "you" language.** Full donor-centring can tip into flattery ("YOU make miracles happen"). The correct register is *partnership*: "you can help build," "partner with us," "join what God is already doing" (TEAM's framing). Keep the mechanic — reduce the institutional "we" — without the hype.
- **Guilt, pressure, or desperation.** Named as the primary failure mode by every church source. No "we can't do this without you," no implied shame, no crisis framing. The congregation has already given sacrificially; the story is momentum, not emergency.
- **Emotional manipulation of beneficiaries.** Secular donation-page research pushes "tragic event" intros and vivid suffering. Wrong genre entirely. The emotional engine here is a 30-year wait and answered prayer, not distress.
- **Emoji in subject lines.** Recommended by general nonprofit email guidance; skip it for this audience and register.

## 2. The email

### Mechanics to use

- **Length: 150–250 words.** Church-letter guidance is one page or less, three to five short paragraphs, comprehensible in one to two minutes. Email should be tighter still — its only job is the click.
- **Subject line: 60 characters or fewer**, roughly 6–10 words. Specificity and curiosity outperform generic appeals. Personalisation (recipient first name) lifts open rates ~26% where the data supports it; if the merge field isn't reliable, go donor-centric instead of risking "Dear ,".
  - Directional examples (test, don't copy): *"Thirty years in a rented hotel"* · *"Prague 6 is finally getting a sanctuary"* · *"A permanent home for our church in Prague"*
  - Avoid: ALL CAPS, "Free," multiple exclamation marks, spam triggers.
- **Sender name matters as much as the subject.** Send from a named human — the pastor or a named missionary contact — not "CB Dejvice Communications." For a warm in-network list this is the highest-leverage variable.
- **Structure** (church-letter order, compressed):
  1. Warm personal opening; where relevant, gratitude for existing relationship.
  2. One-line hook: 30 years, rented hotel, permission finally granted.
  3. Statement of need — what is being built and why, in 2–3 sentences.
  4. The numbers: 54M total, 14M committed by members, 40M remaining.
  5. The ask, direct and unembarrassed, with a link.
  6. Sign with a real name and title.
- **CTA: one primary link, repeated 2–3 times** across the email (inline hyperlink early, button mid, text link in the sign-off/P.S.). Same destination, same words each time.
- **Use a P.S.** It is among the highest-read elements of any fundraising email. Restate the single ask and the link.
- **Attach or link the brochure PDF**, but the P.S./primary CTA should point to the landing page, not the PDF.

### Tone to avoid

- **Do not apologise for asking.** TEAM's guidance is explicit: an invitation to participate is a gift, not an imposition. "Sorry to bother you about money" undercuts the whole chain. Equally, do not be coy — the sources note that many ministry letters tell the story and then never actually ask.
- **Do not fake intimacy at scale.** For a warm list, restrained warmth reads as genuine; over-familiarity reads as a template.
- **No "churchy" performance language.** TEAM: write like you talk; readers detect insincerity.

## 3. The one-page brochure (PDF + QR)

### Mechanics to use

- **Sections, in this order** (adapted from Abstract Union's case-statement framework, compressed to one page):
  1. **Headline + subhead** — vision in one line. Use the existing "Building for the Next Generation / A Healthy and Growing Church for Our Neighbors."
  2. **The story** — founded 1995 after communism; 150 members; Prague 6 has 109,000 residents and 38,000 students with little exposure to the Gospel.
  3. **The problem** — building full all week, congregation relocates to a rented communist-era hotel every Sunday. This is the most vivid and most transferable fact in the whole packet. Lead the problem with it.
  4. **The plan** — sanctuary, largely underground due to Prague regulations, uniting all ministry under one roof. State the underground constraint plainly; it explains the cost and pre-empts the obvious objection.
  5. **The benefits** — keep as a short bulleted list (the existing five bullets, trimmed to 4).
  6. **The financials** — a visual block, not a paragraph: cost / committed / remaining, CZK and USD.
  7. **The ask + QR code + URL.**
- **Skimmable-first layout.** Assume the reader reads headline, financial block, and CTA only. Every one of those three must stand alone.
- **Imagery must earn its place.** Abstract Union: photography or illustration only where it "genuinely complements the text" — nothing decorative. Prioritise the architectural rendering and one photo of the congregation in the current rented space.
- **Consider a giving ladder** (e.g. what $500 / $5,000 / $50,000 represents against the 40M gap). Church capital-campaign practice recommends a visual "giving opportunities" menu; suggested amounts reduce decision fatigue when framed as options rather than expectations. *This is a client call — flag it, don't assume it.*
- **QR code:** place near the CTA, at least 2cm square, with the plain URL printed beneath it for anyone reading a printed copy or unwilling to scan.

### Tone to avoid

- **Naming rights and donor-recognition tiers** are standard capital-campaign furniture but can read as transactional for a small Czech congregation. Do not introduce them without explicit client direction.
- **Triumphalist or corporate framing.** No "world-class facility," no "investment opportunity," no ROI language. The source text's register — "we believe this challenge is part of God's provision" — is the ceiling for confidence and should not be exceeded.

## 4. The landing page

### Mechanics to use

- **Strip the navigation.** NextAfter's single largest tested finding: removing header nav and secondary donate links, leaving only the logo, produced a 195.1% increase in donations (Experiment #4903). Give the page one exit: the donation form.
- **Headline formula:** state the specific impact, not the organisation. Directionally: *"You can help build a permanent home for the church in Prague 6."* Clarified impact headlines tested at +21.2% (Experiment #4164).
- **Intro copy: maximum 6 sentences**, containing the problem and 2–3 specific outcomes. Adding a short intro of exactly this shape tested at +28% (Experiment #900); a story-led version at +150.2% (Experiment #6623).
- **Body copy: maximum 6 sentences**, explaining what the church will do with the money and what the donor's role is. Adding this doubled conversion in one test (2.5% → 6.3%, Experiment #6623). Do not exceed it — landing-page copy is not the brochure.
- **CTA above the fold, in a contrasting colour, repeated** as the reader scrolls. Fundraise Up: the visitor should never have to look for it.
- **No video.** NextAfter removed video from donation pages in three separate experiments and saw conversion increases of 560%, 203%, and 342% (#5287, #3970, #1985). Video breaks donation momentum. If a video exists, put it below the form or on a separate page.
- **Trust signals near the form:** transparency about how funds are used, the 14M CZK already committed by members (powerful social proof — insiders gave first), the cb6.cz link, and a named contact.
- **Mobile-first.** Mobile is the majority of US donation traffic and converts lower than desktop (~8% vs ~11%); sub-3-second load, minimal fields, one-tap wallets (Apple Pay / Google Pay / PayPal) close most of the gap.
- **Currency clarity.** A US donor giving to a Czech church needs to know, above the fold, what currency they are charged in, whether the gift is tax-deductible in the US, and via what mechanism. Missing this will kill conversion regardless of copy quality. **Open question for the client — flag it.**

### Tone to avoid

- **Urgency widgets** — countdown timers, "only X days left," red strikethrough goal numbers. A live progress thermometer showing 14M/54M is acceptable and honest; a deadline clock is not.
- **Popups and exit-intent overlays.** Standard conversion practice; inappropriate for a church asking a warm network.
- **Personalised name-injection in headlines** ("Alex, you make this possible"). Tested well in secular contexts; reads as surveillance-marketing to this audience.

## 5. The donation CTA

### Mechanics to use

- **One CTA, one destination, everywhere in the chain.** Consistent wording across email, PDF, and page.
- **Action verb + object, sentence case.** *"Give to the building fund"* · *"Help build the sanctuary"* · *"Make a gift toward the 40 million."* Avoid the bare "Donate" — action-plus-outcome outperforms generic labels.
- **Minimal form fields.** Name, email, payment. Every additional field costs conversions; ~88% of email clickers never complete the gift, and form friction is a primary cause.
- **Offer suggested amounts and a recurring option.** Preset tiers plus an open "other" field. A monthly opt-in is a single checkbox and materially raises lifetime value.
- **Ask more than once per asset,** but always with the same words and the same link (NextAfter: "ask multiple times; make the action and reasoning crystal clear").
- **Plan the thank-you now.** Church guidance: acknowledgement within days, restating the specific impact of the gift. Write it as part of this sprint, not after.

### Tone to avoid

- **Pressure microcopy** near the button — "Don't leave them waiting," "Last chance." Nothing that implies the reader has failed by hesitating.
- **False deadlines** on the form.

## 6. Quick checklist before anything ships

- [ ] Same governing phrase appears in subject line, brochure headline, and page headline.
- [ ] CZK and USD figures paired everywhere; all four numbers (54M / 14M / 40M / 150 members) consistent across assets.
- [ ] Subject line ≤60 characters, no emoji, no caps, no exclamation marks.
- [ ] Email ≤250 words, real human sender, P.S. with the link.
- [ ] Brochure readable from headline + financial block + CTA alone.
- [ ] QR code with printed URL beneath it.
- [ ] Landing page: no nav, no video, intro ≤6 sentences, body ≤6 sentences, CTA above the fold.
- [ ] Zero instances of guilt, manufactured urgency, or apology for asking.
- [ ] The ask is actually, explicitly made in every asset.
- [ ] Currency / tax-deductibility mechanism for US donors is resolved and stated.
